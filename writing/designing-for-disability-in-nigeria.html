Inclusive Design

Designing for Disability in Nigeria

September 2023 5 min read

Nigeria has an estimated 29 million people living with some form of disability. That is roughly the population of Ghana. And almost none of them were considered when the products, spaces, and interfaces that run daily life in this country were designed.

This is not a design problem. It is a values problem that expresses itself as a design problem.

The Gaps Are Not Hard to See

Walk into any bank in Lagos. Count the accessible entrances. Ride any commercial bus — a danfo, a BRT — and ask yourself how someone with a mobility impairment boards it, pays the fare, finds a seat, gets off at the right stop. Try to use a government portal with a screen reader. Try to find a doctor's form that accommodates patients who cannot write.

The gaps are not subtle. They are architectural, digital, procedural, and cultural, all at once. And they are largely invisible to the people who design Nigerian products and services — because those people, for the most part, are not disabled, and do not know anyone who is, or at least do not know it.

The Cultural Layer

Physical and digital infrastructure failures would be fixable with policy and budget. The cultural layer is harder. In many Nigerian communities, disability carries social stigma that pushes disabled people out of public life — into homes, into silence, into invisibility. A person who isn't seen is easy to forget when decisions get made.

This invisibility compounds itself. Designers design for the users they know. If the users they know do not include people with visual impairments, motor differences, or cognitive disabilities, those users will not appear in the design process. And they will not appear in the finished product. And the finished product will confirm, quietly, that they were never expected to show up.

Inclusive design is not a favour extended to users at the margins. It is an admission that your mental model of "the user" was always too narrow.

The Digital Urgency

Nigeria's digital economy is moving fast. Mobile money, healthtech, fintech, e-commerce — these are not experimental. They are how large portions of the population now access money, medicine, and markets. And almost none of it is built with accessibility in mind.

WCAG 2.1 AA compliance — the international baseline for digital accessibility — is not a feature Nigerian product teams tend to ship. Alt text on images, sufficient colour contrast, keyboard navigability, screen-reader-compatible forms: these are treated as optional extras, luxuries for markets with regulatory enforcement. Nigeria has no regulatory enforcement. So the extras stay optional.

The consequence is that millions of Nigerians are effectively locked out of the digital economy by design decisions that cost very little to make differently.

What Designers Here Can Actually Do

The argument for inclusive design in Nigeria often gets trapped in the language of aspiration: "we should be more inclusive," "the industry needs to do better." This is true and useless. What matters is the specific, practical next step.

Run colour contrast checks as a standard part of your QA process. Tools like WebAIM's contrast checker take thirty seconds. Insufficient contrast fails users with low vision; it is also the most common accessibility error on Nigerian digital products.

Add alt text to every image in your designs — not as an afterthought, but as part of the component spec. If you're building a design system, bake accessibility annotations into it so the expectation is structural, not individual.

Interview disabled users. Not one. Several. People with different conditions, different devices, different literacy levels. If budget makes this impossible, partner with disability organisations: United Cerebral Palsy Nigeria, the Joint National Association of Persons With Disabilities, Disability Rights Advocacy Centre. These organisations exist, and they will engage a designer who comes in good faith.

Make the business case to your clients. Accessible products serve more users. Accessible digital products rank better in search results. Accessible brand communication reaches audiences that exclusionary communication misses. The argument is not only ethical; it is commercial. Both arguments are available. Use whichever opens the door.

The Foundation, Not the Feature

The framing that most Nigerian designers receive — if they receive any framing at all — is that accessibility is a feature: something you add if there's time and budget after the "real" design is finished. This framing is wrong, and it is expensive in the long run. Retrofitting accessibility into a product is harder and more costly than building it in from the beginning.

More fundamentally: a product that excludes 29 million people is not a finished product. It is an incomplete one. Inclusive design is not a charitable addition to a brief. It is the correct scope of the brief — one that many Nigerian designers are not yet being asked to meet, but that the best of them can start demanding.

The work of inclusive design in Nigeria is not glamorous. It is constraint sheets and contrast ratios, interview transcripts and second-guessed assumptions. But it is also the most honest version of what design is for: making the things that people need to use, actually usable, by as many of the people as possible.
